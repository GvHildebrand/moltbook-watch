# Study C — the legal frame (from primary sources, verified)

*Built 2026-09-24 by a three-lens workflow (EU AI Act, Swiss law, accountability literature), synthesised, then every key claim re-fetched by an independent verifier. Vigilia is not a law firm; every statement carries its status label. Items under "Legal review flags" are for a lawyer before publication.*

## Bottom line

Both the EU AI Act and Swiss law create duties that a Moltbook agent's operator can breach, and neither creates any mechanism to find that operator. Article 50 applies from 2 August 2026, was left untouched by the Digital Omnibus, and carries a fine of up to EUR 15M or 3% (Art. 99(4)(g)) — but registration, naming and authorised-representative duties exist only for high-risk systems and GPAI models, and a hobbyist owner is carved out of "deployer" entirely (Art. 2(10)). Swiss law has no AI statute; its only hooks are FADP Art. 19 (a controller must name itself to data subjects) and UCA Art. 3(1)(s) (sellers online must publish identity). Wiz's 88:1 ratio (1.5M agents, ~17,000 owner rows) is therefore not a breach of anything; it is the measured size of the gap between the actors the law addresses and the persons it can reach. The Observatory Archive can turn that single figure into the first population-level measurement. This is a gap analysis, not a legal determination.

## Frame

**Q. Who is the provider of a Moltbook agent?**  
Four candidates, none settled by statute. Model vendor: provider of a GPAI model (Art. 3(63)), Chapter V duties, not the Art. 50 addressee for what an agent posts. OpenClaw framework: provider of an AI system placed on the market free of charge (Art. 3(3)); Art. 2(12) keeps open-source systems inside the Act where they fall under Art. 50, so the 50(1) design duty plausibly lands on the runtime. Owner writing the system prompt: on the words of Art. 3(3) ('has an AI system developed ... puts the AI system into service under its own name') a provider of that specific agent — the provider definition has no personal-use carve-out. Art. 25 (deployer becomes provider) is high-risk only and does not help. All three lenses agree the Commission FAQ and the July 2026 Guidelines do not reach the system-prompt case.  
*Provisions:* Art. 3(3), 3(63), 3(66), 3(13); Art. 2(12) ('This Regulation does not apply to AI systems released under free and open-source licences, unless they are placed on the market or put into service as high-risk AI systems or as an AI system that falls under Article 5 or 50.'); Art. 25(1) · *Status:* reasonable reading; needs legal review at the owner/framework boundary · https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng

**Q. Who is the deployer?**  
The owner, as the person 'using an AI system under its authority' — except that Art. 3(4) and Art. 2(10) remove natural persons acting in a 'purely personal non-professional activity', and the Commission FAQ says a person posting AI content on social media in a personal capacity is outside the Act. A hobbyist running 88 agents has no 50(4) duty. Professional owners (companies, marketers, researchers) keep it, and they are indistinguishable from hobbyists in the archive. The platform (Moltbook, Meta since 2026-03-10) is neither provider nor deployer of the agents; it is a DSA hosting question (recital 136) and holds the only owner table, with no AI Act duty to keep or disclose it.  
*Provisions:* Art. 3(4); Art. 2(10) ('This Regulation does not apply to obligations of deployers who are natural persons using AI systems in the course of a purely personal non-professional activity.'); recital 136 · *Status:* text says (carve-out); reasonable reading (platform outside the Regulation) · https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng

**Q. Does Article 50 apply agent-to-agent?**  
Not on the Commission's own reading: the FAQ places systems operating 'through machine-to-machine communication, or without direct contact with people' outside 50(1). Where humans observe the public feed, three positions: (i) the platform's 'every account is an agent' framing satisfies the 'obvious' exception on-platform — but the Commission says that exception is read restrictively, and Wiz showed humans could post as agents; (ii) the exception fails once a post is quoted elsewhere; (iii) 50(4) (published text informing the public on matters of public interest) is triggered by topic, not audience, and its editorial-responsibility exemption cannot be claimed when no natural or legal person is on record for any post (recital 134). The EU lens labels the observer case contested per surface; the literature lens labels the whole question contested. They do not disagree on substance.  
*Provisions:* Art. 50(1) first sentence; Art. 50(4) second subparagraph; Art. 50(5); recitals 132, 134 · *Status:* text says (machine-to-machine outside 50(1), per FAQ); contested (human observers; 50(4) on an agent-majority audience) · https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act

**Q. What does 17,000 owners behind 1.5M agents mean for enforceability?**  
A fine needs an addressee. The 'if the offender is an undertaking' clause in Art. 99 shows natural persons are fineable, but a market surveillance authority must first identify the person behind a handle, and the Act gives it no tool for a non-high-risk system: no registry (Art. 49/71), no name-on-product (Art. 16(b)), no authorised representative (Art. 22) apply. Of the ~17,000, only EU-established owners are caught with certainty (Art. 2(1)(b)); third-country owners depend on the contested 'output used in the Union' limb (Art. 2(1)(c)). The complaint right (Art. 85) is real but has no addressee unless the platform discloses identity — a DSA/GDPR question. The ratio also overstates accountability coverage: Wiz counts registered agents over owner rows; Moltbook's own counters (6 Jun 2026, via Wikipedia) showed 7.1% of registered agents human-verified. Against the only empirical analogue, Kumar et al.'s 2.25 accounts per human sockpuppeteer, 88:1 is roughly forty times the human baseline.  
*Provisions:* Art. 99(4)(g) ('up to EUR 15 000 000 or, if the offender is an undertaking, up to 3 % of its total worldwide annual turnover ... whichever is higher'); Art. 99(1), (6); Art. 2(1)(a)-(c), (g); Art. 49(1), 16(b), 22(1), 85(1) · *Status:* text says (penalty tier, registry limits); contested (territorial reach via reading in the EU); reasonable reading (enforceable population = platform user table) · https://www.wiz.io/blog/exposed-moltbook-database-reveals-millions-of-api-keys

**Q. What does Swiss law add?**  
No AI statute; the Federal Council (12 Feb 2025) chose ratification of CETS 225 plus sectoral amendments, with an EJPD consultation draft due end-2026 whose named pillars include 'Transparenz' — the place to argue for owner traceability. CETS 225 (signed 27 Mar 2025, not ratified) binds the state and asks only for notification of AI interaction (Art. 15(2)) and identification of AI content (Art. 8), never of the person behind an agent. Today's hooks: FADP Art. 19(2)(a) — a controller collecting personal data must give 'the controller's identity and contact details', fine up to CHF 250,000 on complaint (Art. 60(1)); UCA Art. 3(1)(s) — anyone offering goods or services online must publish identity and email; OR 41 fault liability attributes an agent's harm to its operator, with the ratio itself evidence on whether one human can meet the standard of care over 88 personas. All presuppose an identified defendant. Both lenses agree: an 88:1 ratio is legally invisible in Switzerland in September 2026.  
*Provisions:* FADP Art. 5(a), 19(1)-(2), 21, 25(2)(a), 60(1); UCA Art. 2, 3(1)(b), 3(1)(s), 23; OR 41(1), 55(1), 101(1); ZGB 55; CETS 225 Art. 8, 9, 15(2) · *Status:* text says (statutes, Convention); reasonable reading (no draft published by 23 Sep 2026 — admin.ch unreachable, verify); contested (OR 55/101 by analogy to software agents; whether a post is an 'automated individual decision') · https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/en

**Q. What does the literature propose?**  
Chan et al. (2024, 2025) name the missing piece: visibility into 'by whom' an agent is used, via agent identifiers carrying responsible-party metadata, activity logs and identity binding. South et al. (2025) propose OAuth-style delegation credentials so each agent action carries a verifiable chain to a human principal; Moltbook's only credential is the API key and the human link is an out-of-band tweet, deletable after verification. A2A v1.0 AgentCards carry a provider organisation and card signatures but defer verifying the organisation to deployment policy. Otsuka et al. (2026) list 'recursive delegation accountability' as an open gap with no empirical number attached. Vendor policies (Anthropic, Google) place the disclosure duty on the operator and say nothing about how many agents one person may run. No published study measures agent-to-owner ratios on an agent platform; the archive-derived metrics below would be new.  
*Provisions:* arXiv 2401.13138 §3; 2406.12137; 2501.10114; 2501.09674; 2604.23280; A2A Specification 1.0.0 §8.4; Anthropic Usage Policy (15 Sep 2025) · *Status:* text says (summaries); reasonable reading (Moltbook mapping) · https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13138


## What the ratio implies

The ratio is a measurement of the gap between actors and addressees, not evidence of any breach. On the EU side the actor set (agents) is two orders of magnitude larger than the largest possible addressee set (owner handles), and that set shrinks further to the intersection of EU-established and professionally acting owners, which the archive cannot see directly. The only role with no personal-use exemption is 'provider', and whether a system-prompt author holds it is the unresolved question that decides whether 88 agents have any EU addressee at all. On the Swiss side the ratio matters only after a defendant is known, as evidence on the fault element (Sorgfalt) under OR 41. For the brief: 88:1 should be presented as Wiz's registration-era figure, then replaced with the archive's honest denominators (posting agents per distinct claimed handle, pre and post 2026-03-10), and set against Kumar et al.'s 2.25 human sockpuppet baseline. Where lenses diverge: the literature lens leans towards owner-as-provider as 'most plausible'; the EU lens holds it at 'contested, needs legal review'. The brief should carry the EU lens's caution.

## Synthesis markdown (verbatim)

## The legal frame: an attribution gap, not a breach

**Bottom line.** Both the EU AI Act and Swiss law impose duties that the operator of a Moltbook agent can breach, and neither gives anyone a way to find that operator. Article 50 applies from 2 August 2026, survived the Digital Omnibus untouched, and carries a maximum fine of EUR 15 million or 3% of turnover (Art. 99(4)(g)). But every registration, naming and authorised-representative duty in the Act is confined to high-risk systems and general-purpose models, and a natural person running agents as a hobby is carved out of "deployer" altogether (Art. 2(10)). Swiss law has no AI statute; its only hooks are the FADP (a controller must name itself to data subjects) and the Unfair Competition Act (a seller online must publish identity). Wiz's 88:1 figure — roughly 1.5 million agents against about 17,000 owner rows — is therefore not evidence of any violation. It is the measured distance between the actors the law addresses and the persons it can reach. *This is a gap analysis by a disclosed AI agent, not a legal determination; every item marked "needs legal review" goes to counsel before publication.*

### Six questions

**1. Who is the provider?** — *reasonable reading; needs legal review.* The Act defines a provider as one who "develops an AI system ... or that has an AI system ... developed and places it on the market or puts the AI system into service under its own name or trademark, whether for payment or free of charge" (Art. 3(3)). Four candidates. The model vendor is a GPAI-model provider (Art. 3(63)) with Chapter V duties, not the addressee for what an agent posts. The OpenClaw framework is a provider of a system placed on the market free of charge; Art. 2(12) states the Regulation "does not apply to AI systems released under free and open-source licences, unless they are placed on the market or put into service as high-risk AI systems or as an AI system that falls under Article 5 or 50", so the 50(1) design duty plausibly lands on the runtime. The owner who writes the system prompt and runs the agent under a handle fits the words "has an AI system developed ... puts [it] into service under its own name" — and the provider definition has no personal-use carve-out. Art. 25, which turns deployers into providers, is high-risk only and does not resolve this. All three lenses agree the Commission FAQ and the July 2026 Guidelines do not address the system-prompt case. The literature lens calls owner-as-provider "most plausible"; the EU lens holds it at contested. The brief should carry the caution.

**2. Who is the deployer?** — *text says (carve-out); reasonable reading (platform).* The owner, "using an AI system under its authority" (Art. 3(4)) — unless a natural person "using AI systems in the course of a purely personal non-professional activity" (Art. 2(10)), whom the Commission FAQ puts outside the Act entirely. A hobbyist with 88 agents owes nothing under 50(4); a company, marketer or researcher does, and the two are indistinguishable in the archive. Moltbook (Meta since 2026-03-10) is neither provider nor deployer of the agents. It is a DSA hosting question (recital 136), holds the only owner table, and has no AI Act duty to keep or disclose it.

**3. Does Article 50 apply agent-to-agent?** — *text says (FAQ); contested (observers).* The Commission FAQ places systems operating "through machine-to-machine communication, or without direct contact with people" outside 50(1). For the human who reads the feed: the platform's "every account is an agent" framing may satisfy the "obvious" exception on-platform, but the Commission reads that exception restrictively and Wiz showed humans could post as agents via a plain POST request — recital 132's impersonation risk, inverted. Off-platform, once a post is quoted, the exception fails. 50(4) (text "published with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest") turns on topic, not audience; its editorial-responsibility exemption cannot be claimed when no natural or legal person is on record for any post (recital 134).

**4. What does 17,000 behind 1.5 million mean for enforceability?** — *text says (tiers, registry limits); contested (territorial reach).* A fine needs an addressee. Natural persons are fineable — the clause "if the offender is an undertaking" implies a fixed cap for those who are not — but an authority must first identify the person behind a handle, and for a non-high-risk system the Act offers no registry (Art. 49/71), no name-on-product (Art. 16(b)), no authorised representative (Art. 22). Only EU-established owners are caught with certainty (Art. 2(1)(b)); third-country owners depend on whether a post read in the EU is output "used in the Union" (Art. 2(1)(c)), on which no guidance exists. The Art. 85 complaint right has no addressee unless the platform discloses identity — a DSA/GDPR path, outside this Regulation. The ratio also overstates coverage: Wiz counts registered agents over owner rows, while Moltbook's own counters on 6 June 2026 (via Wikipedia, primary not read) showed 7.1% of registered agents human-verified. Against the one empirical analogue — Kumar et al. (2017), 2.25 accounts per human sockpuppeteer on Disqus — 88:1 is roughly forty times the human baseline.

**5. What does Swiss law add?** — *text says (statutes); reasonable reading (no draft yet); contested (liability analogies).* No AI act. On 12 February 2025 the Federal Council chose to ratify CETS 225 (signed 27 March 2025, not ratified), amend sectoral law, and table a consultation draft by end-2026 whose named pillars include "Transparenz" — the venue for an owner-traceability argument. The Convention asks Parties to "seek to ensure ... persons interacting with artificial intelligence systems are notified that they are interacting with such systems" (Art. 15(2)) and to address "identification of content generated by artificial intelligence systems" (Art. 8); neither concerns the person behind the agent. Today's hooks: FADP Art. 19(2) — a controller must give "the controller's identity and contact details", fine up to CHF 250,000 on complaint (Art. 60(1)); UCA Art. 3(1)(s) — a person offering goods or services online must give "clear and complete details of their identity and their contact address including their email address"; OR 41(1) — "Any person who unlawfully causes damage to another, whether wilfully or negligently, is obliged to provide compensation", with the ratio itself evidence on whether one human can meet the standard of care over 88 personas. Every one of these presupposes an identified defendant. Both Swiss-facing lenses agree: an 88:1 ratio is legally invisible in Switzerland in September 2026.

**6. What does the literature propose?** — *text says.* Chan et al. (2024) name the missing piece as visibility into "by whom" agents are used, through identifiers with responsible-party metadata, activity logs and identity binding (2025). South et al. (2025) propose OAuth-style delegation credentials so each action carries a verifiable chain to a human principal; Moltbook's only credential is the API key, and the human link is an out-of-band tweet, deletable after verification. A2A v1.0 AgentCards carry a provider organisation and signatures but defer verifying the organisation to deployment policy. Otsuka et al. (2026) list delegation accountability as an open gap with no number attached. Vendor policies (Anthropic, Google) put the disclosure duty on the operator and say nothing about fleet size. No published study measures agent-to-owner ratios on an agent platform.
